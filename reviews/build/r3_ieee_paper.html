Numerical Reasoning over Financial Reports: Evaluating Prompting Strategies, Automated Prompt Optimization and Verified Program Self-Consistency with Local LLMs

Abstract—Answering analytical questions over financial reports requires locating evidence in tables and text, reading values with the right unit, scale and sign, and performing multi-step arithmetic — tasks at which Large Language Models (LLMs) remain unreliable. We present a controlled, cost-aware evaluation of prompting strategies for this task with small local models. On FinQA, the arithmetic subset of TAT-QA and a new set of 44 questions over Indian annual reports (₹ crore/lakh), we compare direct answering, zero- and few-shot Chain-of-Thought, decomposition, Program-of-Thought, Self-Consistency, automated optimization with DSPy and TextGrad, and a new method, Verified Program Self-Consistency (VPSC), which discards generated programs whose numbers do not occur in the report before voting. With Qwen3-1.7B, direct answering reaches at most 11% while reasoning strategies reach 57%, 73% and 77% on the three sets; Program-of-Thought is the best single-call strategy on FinQA and Indian reports (CoT few-shot on TAT-QA), and five-sample strategies cost 4.4–5.0× the tokens of few-shot CoT without significant gains. 60% of the remaining errors are evidence errors. Grounding verification flags these answers precisely but rarely changes the vote of a small model.

Index Terms—large language models, prompt engineering, prompt optimization, program-of-thought, financial question answering, numerical reasoning

I. Introduction

Financial analysts routinely ask numerical questions of annual reports: growth rates, margins, ratios, compound growth. Although LLMs are fluent in financial language, their numerical reasoning over reports is unreliable: on FinQA the best fine-tuned model of the original paper reached 61.24% execution accuracy against 91.16% for experts [1], and GPT-4-Turbo with retrieval failed on 81% of evaluated FinanceBench questions [5]. Prompting methods — Chain-of-Thought (CoT) [10], [11], Self-Consistency [12], Program-of-Thought (PoT) [15], [16] — and prompt optimizers — DSPy [19], MIPROv2 [20], TextGrad [21] — improve reasoning without training, but have not been compared under one protocol, with cost and significance, on financial table-text reasoning with small local models that keep sensitive data on-premise.

We contribute (i) a controlled benchmark of eleven strategies with confidence intervals, paired tests and token cost; (ii) VPSC, a verification-based variant of program self-consistency with an exactly matched ablation; (iii) a new Indian annual-report test set with ₹ crore/lakh units and formula-computed, PDF-verified gold answers; and (iv) an error taxonomy, a numerical-perturbation test and an auditable application.

II. Related Work

Benchmarks. FinQA [1] provides 8,281 expert-annotated questions with reasoning programs over S&P 500 earnings reports; TAT-QA [2] provides 16,552 questions over hybrid table-text contexts; ConvFinQA [3], DocFinQA [4] and FinanceBench [5] extend to conversations, full documents and open-book QA. Srivastava et al. [8] evaluate LLM mathematical reasoning on these datasets and propose EEDP prompting. Prompting. CoT [10] and zero-shot CoT [11] elicit intermediate steps; Self-Consistency [12] votes over sampled paths; decomposition methods [13], [14], [35] split problems; PoT and PAL [15], [16] delegate arithmetic to an interpreter, raising Codex's FinQA accuracy from 40.4% to 64.5% [15]. Optimization. APE [17] and OPRO [18] search instructions with LLMs; DSPy [19] compiles declarative programs, MIPROv2 [20] jointly optimizes instructions and demonstrations, and TextGrad [21] back-propagates textual feedback. Robustness. Table serialization affects LLM accuracy [22], and changing only the numbers of math problems lowers accuracy [28]. Evidence retrieval combines BM25 [23], Sentence-BERT embeddings [24] in FAISS [25] and reciprocal rank fusion [26], as in retrieval-augmented generation [27]; Binder [34] binds LMs to symbolic languages over tables. Domain models. BloombergGPT [6] and TAT-LLM [7] train or fine-tune finance-specific models, whereas we study in-context learning [9] with general models. Following Dror et al. [32], we report paired significance tests. Unlike prior work, we test whether a program's numbers are grounded in the source document.

III. Method

A. Task and Scoring

Given a table T, text P and question q, the system outputs a number (or yes/no). A prediction is correct if it is within 0.5% of the gold value or equals it at the gold's precision; for percentage questions both percent and fraction forms are accepted, since FinQA stores fractions and TAT-QA percentages.

B. Strategies

All strategies share the same system prompt and answer-format rules, and few-shot variants use the same three FinQA-train examples. PoT programs run in a sandbox (AST checks, isolated interpreter, builtin whitelist, CPU and time limits) with one repair round. DSPy compiles a Chain-of-Thought program whose metric is our scorer; TextGrad optimizes the system prompt of zero-shot CoT, keeping an update only if validation accuracy does not drop. All optimization uses FinQA train/dev only.

C. Verified Program Self-Consistency

VPSC samples five programs (one greedy, four at T = 0.7), executes them, and keeps a program only if every numeric literal in its syntax tree occurs in the report, allowing small integers, unit constants (102, 103, 105, 106, 107, 109), years and ×100/×1000 rescaling. The answer is the majority over kept programs (all executed programs if none is kept). PoT-SC votes over the same five programs without the check, isolating its effect.

Algorithm 1: VPSC(q, T, P)
 1: C ← serialize(T) ⊕ P;  N ← numbers(C)
 2: for i = 0..4: code_i ← LLM(PoT-prompt(C, q), T = 0 if i = 0 else 0.7)
 3:   (ok_i, a_i) ← sandbox(code_i)
 4:   g_i ← ∀ x ∈ literals(code_i): x ∈ const ∨ ∃ n ∈ N, s ∈ S: x = n·s
 5: K ← {i : ok_i ∧ g_i};  if K = ∅: K ← {i : ok_i}
 6: return mode{a_i : i ∈ K}, confidence = votes / 5

IV. Experimental Setup

We evaluate Qwen3-1.7B [29] (4-bit, Ollama, non-thinking mode) on fixed samples of 100 FinQA and 100 TAT-QA arithmetic test questions (seed 42) and on all 44 Indian questions, built from the statements of Titan, Asian Paints, Marico and Pidilite; each gold answer is the sandboxed result of a formula whose operands were checked against the PDF page. Uncertainty is a 95% bootstrap CI [31]; strategies are compared with few-shot CoT using exact McNemar tests [30] with Holm correction [33].

Main results. Showing work is essential. Direct answering reaches only 2–11%; the best reasoning strategy reaches 57% (Program-of-Thought) on FinQA, 73% (CoT few-shot) on TAT-QA, 77% (Program-of-Thought) on Indian reports. Program-of-Thought vs few-shot CoT: FinQA +5 pts (Holm p = 1.000); TAT-QA -6 pts (Holm p = 1.000); Indian reports +11 pts (Holm p = 1.000). None of these differences is significant after Holm correction — with 44–100 questions only large effects can be detected.

Cost. Self-Consistency is poor value for a small model: five CoT samples change accuracy by +1 pts on FinQA, -1 pts on TAT-QA, +0 pts on Indian reports at about 5.0× the tokens of one few-shot CoT call — none of these differences is significant. Fig. 1 shows zero-shot CoT and PoT on the cost-efficiency frontier.

Errors. Evidence errors dominate: across reasoning strategies on FinQA and TAT-QA, 60% of the 549 wrong answers use a wrong or missing operand, 23% have all operands but a wrong formula/arithmetic, 9% are unit/scale errors and 5% sign errors. The bottleneck is finding and copying the right numbers, not computing.

VPSC. VPSC (ours): the grounding check rejected 26 of 1203 executed programs (2.2%), touching 6 questions; 6 of these 6 questions were answered wrongly. Because the five samples agree strongly (mean vote agreement 91% on FinQA, 97% on TAT-QA, 99% on Indian reports) and in 5 of the 6 flagged questions all programs were ungrounded (so the fallback vote was used), verification changed no final answer: VPSC = PoT-SC. The check is therefore most useful as a reliability flag (an ungrounded answer is very likely wrong) rather than as a vote filter for this model.

VI. Discussion and Limitations

For a small model the decisive choice is whether to reason at all; among reasoning strategies, differences are within the ±10-point CIs of 100-question samples. Programs remove arithmetic errors, leaving evidence selection as the bottleneck. VPSC's grounding check is precise — every flagged question was wrong — but the 1.7B model's samples agree in 91%, 97%, 99% of votes on average, so filtering seldom changes the majority; it is better used to abstain or alert. Limitations: small samples, one model family, a small self-authored Indian set, a heuristic error taxonomy, and value-level (not cell-level) grounding.

VII. Conclusion

We presented a reproducible, cost-aware comparison of prompting and prompt optimization for financial numerical reasoning with local LLMs, a new Indian test set and VPSC. Program-of-Thought is the most cost-effective strong strategy; the remaining errors are evidence errors, which grounding verification flags. Future work: cell-aware grounding with abstention, larger models and whole-document retrieval.
