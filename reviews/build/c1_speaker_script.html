Gaurav Girish

Slide 1 · Title & introductions (~70 s)

Good morning, everyone. We are Gaurav, Sunay, Dhruvagouda and Hanshal. Our project is called FinReason. It is about one simple question: can AI models answer number questions about company annual reports correctly? For example: by how much did a company's revenue grow this year? Large language models, like ChatGPT, are very good with words. But they often make mistakes with numbers. In this project we test different ways of asking the model — we call these prompting strategies — and we find out which way works best. Here is our plan for today. I will explain the problem, why it matters, and our problem statement. Sunay will give our objectives, the literature, three foundational and three latest 2025 to 2026 papers with the gaps we address, and our pipeline. Hanshal will explain our seven prompting strategies with real prompts and code. Finally, Dhruvagouda will cover automatic prompt optimization, our experimental design and what is new in our work.

Slide 2 · Numerical questions over financial reports (~65 s)

Let me start with a real example. This table is from Titan's annual report. All the amounts are in rupees crore. The question is: what was the growth in revenue from FY24 to FY25? A person answers this in four steps. First, find the right row, revenue from operations, and the two year columns. Second, read the numbers correctly — 54,842 and 47,114 — with the right unit. Be careful: a number in brackets, like 6,537, means minus 6,537. Third, pick the right formula: new value minus old value, divided by the old value, times 100. Fourth, do the calculation: the answer is 16.40 percent. If the model makes a mistake in any one of these four steps — one wrong digit, one wrong unit, or one wrong sign — the final answer is wrong. And the worst part is that the model still sounds confident. That is the problem we are working on.

Slide 3 · LLMs read finance well but calculate badly (~60 s)

Why does this matter? Look at these three numbers from other researchers. First, on a well-known test called FinQA, the best computer model scored 61 percent. Human finance experts scored 91 percent. That is a big gap. Second, on another test called FinanceBench, GPT-4-Turbo got 81 percent of the questions wrong or refused to answer. So even big models struggle. Third, there is good news. When the model writes a small Python program instead of plain text, accuracy on FinQA goes up by 24 points. So how we ask the model makes a big difference. We also want to use small models that run on our own laptop. Financial data is private, and companies do not want to send it to outside servers. Changing the prompt is free and fast. That is why we focus on prompting.

Slide 4 · Problem statement (~60 s)

So here is our problem statement in one sentence: we want to find which prompting methods, and which automatic prompt-improvement methods, make AI models accurate, reliable and cheap when answering number questions about financial reports. Look at the three boxes. The input is one page of a report — a table and some text — plus a question. The output is the answer, and also the rows and the calculation that were used, so a person can check it. What we do not do: we do not retrain or fine-tune any model, and we do not search through whole 300-page reports. We keep the model fixed and change only the prompt. Then we measure how accuracy, cost and reliability change. I now hand over to Sunay, who will present the next part, starting with “Objectives”.

Sunay R Hegde

Slide 5 · Objectives (~60 s)

Thank you, Gaurav. We have four objectives. One: compare seven prompting strategies, including our own method, VPSC. For example, asking for the answer directly, asking the model to think step by step, asking it many times and taking a vote, and asking it to write Python code. Two: test tools that improve the prompt automatically. These are DSPy and TextGrad. We want to know if they beat prompts written by hand. Three: compare a small model with 1.7 billion parameters and a bigger one with 8 billion parameters. We look at accuracy and also at cost. Four: study the mistakes the models make, check that the results are robust, and test the models on Indian annual reports, which use crore and lakh. Very few studies have looked at Indian reports, and we found none that tests multi-step calculations on them.

Slide 6 · Financial QA benchmarks (~75 s)

Now our literature survey. A benchmark is a standard set of test questions that everyone uses. These are the main ones for finance. FinQA has 8,281 questions written by finance experts. Each question comes with the exact steps to solve it. The best model got 61 percent, experts got 91. TAT-QA has about 16,500 questions on tables and text from annual reports. The best model got 50 percent exact matches, humans got 84. FinanceBench showed that even GPT-4-Turbo fails most questions about real company filings. And the EEDP paper tested many language models on four financial datasets. It also proposed a prompt that first extracts the numbers and then breaks the calculation into steps. Our decomposition strategy is based on this idea. Very recently, in 2026, two Indian benchmarks also appeared. IndFin-Bench asks mostly look-up questions about Indian company filings, and IndiaFinBench uses regulatory text from SEBI and RBI. Neither focuses on multi-step calculations from annual reports, which is what our Indian set does. The key point: even the best systems are about 30 points below human experts.

Slide 7 · Main and latest papers — and the gaps we address (~90 s)

This slide shows the six papers our project builds on: three foundational ones at the top and three of the latest, from 2025 and 2026, below. FinQA defines our task; its fine-tuned model reached 61 percent against 91 for experts, but it needs fine-tuning and covers only US reports. We use prompting only and add Indian reports. Program-of-Thought lets Python do the arithmetic, from 40 to 64 percent on FinQA, but it trusts every number in the code. Our VPSC rejects numbers that are not in the report. EEDP found most errors come from extracting numbers, but compared only hand-written prompts. We add DSPy and TextGrad and a memorization test. Now the latest work. FinanceReasoning, ACL 2025, is a harder benchmark; even OpenAI o1 writing Python reaches only 89 percent, and it uses only very large models. We test small local models. VeriFin, 2026, checks each number with XBRL data and a logic solver and accepted no wrong claims, but it needs XBRL, which Indian annual-report PDFs do not have. VPSC checks the plain table. IndiaFinBench, 2026, is the newest Indian benchmark, but it uses SEBI and RBI regulatory text and tests models zero-shot only. Our Indian set has multi-step calculations from annual reports, and we compare seven strategies on it.

Slide 8 · One pipeline — only the prompt changes (~60 s)

This picture shows our approach. Every question goes through the same eight steps. Step one, load the question. Step two, turn the table into text the model can read. Step three, build the prompt using one strategy. Step four, the model answers. Step five, if the model wrote code, we run it in a safe sandbox. Step six, we check if the answer is correct. Step seven, if it is wrong, we find out why. Step eight, we save everything for analysis. The important idea is this: the questions, the model and the checking are always the same. Only the prompting strategy changes. So if the results are different, we know the strategy caused it. That makes our comparison fair. Hanshal Nagesh

Slide 9 · Our seven prompting strategies (~60 s)

Thank you, Sunay. We compare seven prompting strategies, from the simplest to our own method. Direct asks only for the answer; it is our baseline. Zero-shot Chain-of-Thought adds one instruction: think step by step. Few-shot Chain-of-Thought also shows three worked examples, taken from the FinQA training set so they never overlap with test questions. Decomposition, based on the EEDP paper, forces four labelled parts: the formula, the extracted values, the steps and the answer. Program-of-Thought asks for Python code, and our sandbox runs it. Self-Consistency samples five answers and takes a vote. VPSC, our method, samples five programs and keeps only those whose numbers are really in the report. Each strategy targets a different kind of error, shown in the last column. On top of these, three automatic optimizers give ten configurations in total.

Slide 10 · Text-based strategies — one question, four prompts (~55 s)

Here is one question asked in four different ways. The question is the revenue growth from FY24 to FY25. With Direct, the model gives only a number, so we cannot see or check how it got it. With zero-shot Chain-of-Thought, it writes the two numbers and the calculation before the answer. Few-shot Chain-of-Thought shows it worked examples first, so it copies the right format, units and rounding. Decomposition is the most structured: formula first, then each value with its year and unit, then one operation per step. All four use the same answer rules, so only the reasoning instruction differs. The key point: the more structure we ask for, the more clearly we can see where each number came from, so errors become visible.

Slide 11 · Code-based strategies — Program-of-Thought → VPSC (~55 s)

Now the code-based strategies. In Program-of-Thought the model writes Python with a named variable for each value, and the sandbox computes the answer. So the computer, not the model, does the arithmetic. If the code fails, we send the error back once so the model can fix it. VPSC builds on this. We sample five programs, run each one, and check every number written in the code. If a number such as 54,482 does not appear in the report, that program is dropped. The remaining programs vote. Our ablation, PoT Self-Consistency, uses the same five programs without the check, so any difference comes from the verification alone. Dhruvagouda

Slide 12 · Optimizing the prompts automatically (~60 s)

Thank you, Hanshal. Besides hand-written prompts, we test three automatic optimizers. DSPy BootstrapFewShot chooses the worked examples: it keeps examples the model solved correctly on training questions. MIPROv2 also rewrites the instruction, searching over instruction and example combinations. TextGrad lets a language model criticise the wrong answers and rewrite the system prompt, and we keep the new prompt only if dev accuracy does not drop. In their papers, DSPy improved over few-shot prompting by more than 25 percent, MIPROv2 by up to 13 percent, and TextGrad raised GPT-4o from 51 to 55 percent on a science test. We use only 50 training and 40 dev FinQA questions, the same scorer as our experiments, and we report their one-time token cost. Our question: can these automatic tools beat carefully written prompts on financial tables?

Slide 13 · Experimental design (~40 s)

This is our full experimental design. The factors are ten prompt configurations, two local Qwen3 models, three datasets, four table formats, full page versus retrieved rows, and thinking mode on or off. We measure accuracy, exact match and F1, tokens per question and per correct answer, latency, and how often the format or code fails. For analysis we use 95 percent confidence intervals, McNemar tests against few-shot Chain-of-Thought, accuracy by number of steps and evidence source, an eight-category error taxonomy, and a test where we change the numbers to detect memorization.

Slide 14 · What is new (~70 s)

What is new in our project? Four things. First, our own method called VPSC. The model writes five programs. We run all five. Then we check every number in each program. If a program uses a number that is not in the report, we throw it away. Then the remaining programs vote on the answer. It is related to recent checkers like VeriFin and DCRC, but it is much lighter: it needs no structured data, no solver and no training. Second, a new test set of 44 calculation questions that we wrote ourselves, from the annual reports of Titan, Asian Paints, Marico and Pidilite. These use crore and lakh, and unlike the recent Indian benchmarks, every question needs a multi-step calculation. Third, a fair head-to-head comparison of hand-written prompts and automatic prompt tools, with cost and statistical tests. Fourth, a web app that shows exactly how every answer was found — the rows used, the code and the votes.
