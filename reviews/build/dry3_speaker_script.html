First, a quick reminder of how we tested. Every question goes through the same eight steps, as shown in this picture. The questions, the model and the checking stay the same. Because everything else is fixed, any difference in the results is caused by the strategy.

Slide 3 · Experimental setup (~45 s)

Here are the details of the setup. We used 100 FinQA questions, 100 TAT-QA questions, all 44 Indian questions, and 88 FinQA questions where we changed the numbers. An answer counts as correct if it is within half a percent of the true answer. Because we only have about 100 questions per dataset, we also show uncertainty ranges. And when we say one strategy is better than another, we check it with a statistical test called McNemar's test. That way we do not claim a difference that could just be luck. Slide 6 · 1.7B vs 8B (~35 s)

Thank you, Sunay. This chart compares the small and the big model. For each strategy, blue is the small 1.7 billion model and orange is the bigger 8 billion model. With Direct, FinQA accuracy goes from 5 percent to 37 percent. With Chain-of-Thought few-shot, FinQA accuracy goes from 52 percent to 70 percent. Slide 7 · Indian annual reports (~35 s)

Now the Indian annual reports. These are new questions that we wrote ourselves, so no model could have seen them before. They are in rupees crore and lakh, they use brackets for negative numbers, and the tables are long. Look at the table. Asking directly gets only 2 percent. Step-by-step reasoning with examples gets 66 percent. And Program-of-Thought, where the model writes code, gets 77 percent. That is even higher than on FinQA, where it got 57 percent. So our methods work on Indian reports too.

Slide 8 · Accuracy vs. cost (~60 s)

Now let's look at cost. A token is a small piece of text — the model reads and writes tokens, and more tokens means more time and more money. On this chart, left means cheaper and up means more accurate. So the best strategies are at the top left. Going from cheapest to most accurate, the best-value strategies on FinQA are Direct, then Chain-of-Thought zero-shot, then Program-of-Thought. On TAT-QA they are Direct, then Chain-of-Thought zero-shot, then Chain-of-Thought few-shot, then Self-Consistency. Every other strategy costs more without being more accurate. Now look at the right side of the chart. These are the strategies that ask the model five times. They use 4.4 to 5.0 times more tokens than asking once, but they are not significantly more accurate. So for a small model, asking five times is not worth the cost.

Slide 9 · Why answers are wrong (~60 s)

Next, we looked at why answers are wrong. For every wrong answer, our program decides the type of mistake using fixed rules. The blue part is the biggest. About 60 percent of mistakes are evidence errors. That means the model used a wrong number, or forgot a number it needed. About 23 percent are reasoning errors: the numbers were right but the formula was wrong. About 9 percent are unit errors, like mixing up millions and thousands, and 4 percent are sign errors, like plus instead of minus. The grey part at the top is the Direct strategy, which usually shows no working at all. So the main problem is not the maths. It is picking the right numbers. Slide 10 · VPSC result (~60 s)

Thank you, Gaurav. Now our own method, VPSC. Remember: the model writes five programs, and we throw away any program that uses a number not found in the report. Here is what happened. Out of 1203 programs that ran, the check threw away 26. These came from 6 questions. Look at the first two columns. VPSC got the same accuracy as voting without the check. Why? Because this small model usually writes the same program five times. So when one program is bad, all five are bad, and throwing them away does not change the vote. But here is the useful part: all 6 answers that the check flagged were wrong. So when the check fires, we know the answer cannot be trusted. We can use it as a warning light for the user.

Slide 11 · DSPy and TextGrad (~20 s)

This slide is for the automatic prompt tools, DSPy and TextGrad. Their experiments run last, and they are still running. When they finish, this slide will show whether the automatically improved prompts beat our hand-written prompts, and how much time and computing it cost to improve them.

Slide 12 · Memorization check: change the numbers (~45 s)

Many of these test questions are public, so a model might have memorized the answers. We tested this. We took 88 FinQA questions and changed every number in them by 10 to 30 percent. Then we calculated the new correct answers. If the model just remembers old answers, it will fail on the new numbers. Look at the table. Chain-of-Thought few-shot went from 56 percent to 50 percent; Program-of-Thought went from 58 percent to 56 percent; VPSC (ours) went from 57 percent to 55 percent. These are small drops, and our statistical test says none of them is significant. So the model is really calculating, not remembering.

Slide 13 · What else changes accuracy? (~65 s)

On this slide we changed one thing at a time, to see what else affects accuracy. First, the table format — how we write the table for the model. On FinQA, JSON worked best with 54 percent, and plain row text worked worst with 44 percent. So the way the table is written matters. Second, we tried giving the model only the five most relevant rows instead of the whole page. On FinQA this lowered accuracy from 57 percent to 51 percent, because sometimes a needed number was left out. Third, Qwen3 has a 'thinking mode', where it thinks for longer before answering. It raised accuracy from 49 percent to 63 percent, and this difference is statistically significant. But it used about 2 times the text and took about 13 times longer. So it helps, but it is slow. Dhruvagouda

Slide 14 · Key findings (~75 s)

Thank you, Hanshal. Let me sum up our key findings in simple words. One: showing the working is essential. Asking for the answer directly gets at most 11 percent. Asking for reasoning gets 44 percent to 77 percent. Two: Program-of-Thought is the best single-call strategy on FinQA and Indian reports (Chain-of-Thought few-shot on TAT-QA). Three: asking five times and voting is not worth it for a small model — five-sample strategies cost 4.4 to 5.0 times the tokens of few-shot Chain-of-Thought without significant gains. Four: the main problem is picking the right numbers. About 60 percent of mistakes come from using a wrong or missing number. Five: our VPSC check is a reliable warning light — every answer it flagged was wrong. Six: when we change the numbers, accuracy does not drop significantly, so the model is not just remembering answers; and the methods also work on Indian reports. And finally, thinking mode helps a lot — 49 percent to 63 percent — but it is much slower.

Slide 17 · Conclusion, limitations, future work (~50 s)

To conclude. First, good prompting makes a small model on a laptop useful: asking for reasoning turns a model that is almost always wrong into one that is right most of the time. Second, letting the model write Python code is the best way to get the calculation right. Third, the main remaining problem is picking the right numbers from the report. And fourth, our grounding check can warn the user when an answer should not be trusted. Our main limitations are the small number of questions, one model family, and that our check does not yet confirm the exact cell. Next we would use full test sets, bigger models and a cell-level check.
