1. Introduction

Financial reports — annual reports, 10-K filings, earnings releases — communicate a company's performance through a mix of tables (statements of profit and loss, balance sheets, multi-year highlights) and narrative text. Analysts routinely ask numerical questions of these documents: How much did revenue grow? What was the operating margin? What is the current ratio, the interest coverage, the five-year CAGR? Answering such a question requires four distinct skills: (i) locating the right evidence among dozens of rows and paragraphs, (ii) extracting values with the correct unit, scale and sign (millions vs. billions, ₹ crore vs. lakh, bracketed negatives), (iii) applying the right domain formula, and (iv) carrying out multi-step arithmetic without error.

Large Language Models (LLMs) write fluent financial prose, but they remain unreliable at this kind of numerical reasoning. On the FinQA benchmark the best fine-tuned model of the original paper reached 61.24% execution accuracy against 91.16% for financial experts [1]; on FinanceBench, GPT-4-Turbo with a retrieval system answered 81% of the evaluated questions incorrectly or refused to answer [5]. Prompt engineering offers a cheap lever: Chain-of-Thought [10], Self-Consistency [12] and Program-of-Thought [15] improve multi-step reasoning without any training, and recent frameworks such as DSPy [19], MIPROv2 [20] and TextGrad [21] promise to optimize prompts automatically. This project, FinReason, systematically measures how far these techniques take small and mid-sized open models on financial numerical reasoning, what they cost, where they fail, and whether a verification step can fix the dominant failure.

2. Problem statement and scope

Problem statement. Given a page of a financial report (a table and its surrounding text) and a natural-language question that requires numerical reasoning, produce the correct numeric answer together with a transparent evidence and calculation trace; and determine, through controlled experiments, which prompting strategies and automated prompt-optimization methods make LLMs most accurate, reliable and cost-efficient at this task — for small local and larger models, on public benchmarks and on unseen Indian annual reports.

2.1 Formal task definition

Each instance is a tuple (T, P, q, a) where T is a table (a header row and data rows), P a list of text sentences or paragraphs, q a question and a the gold answer (a real number or yes/no). A system f maps (T, P, q) to a prediction â. The prediction is correct when it is within 0.5% of a, or equals a rounded to the precision a is written with; for percentage questions both the percent (12.5) and fraction (0.125) forms are accepted, because FinQA stores percentages as fractions while TAT-QA stores them as percent numbers.

2.2 Scope

  • In scope: arithmetic questions over a single report page (FinQA, the arithmetic subset of TAT-QA, our Indian set); six manual prompting strategies; three automated optimizers; two local model sizes (Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-8B [29]), with API models supported by the code; evidence retrieval within the page; robustness and error analysis; a web application.
  • Out of scope: fine-tuning or training models; span-extraction and counting questions; search over whole 300-page reports; real-time market data.

3. Motivation

  • Accuracy matters. A wrong growth rate or ratio silently propagates into investment, credit and audit decisions. The ~30-point gap between the best systems and experts on FinQA and TAT-QA [1], [2] shows the problem is far from solved.
  • Answers must be auditable. Financial users need to see which numbers were used and how they were combined — not only a final number.
  • Privacy and cost favour small local models. Many organizations cannot send unpublished financial data to external APIs. A 2–8B-parameter model running on a laptop costs nothing per query, but its reasoning ability is limited — prompting may be the cheapest way to compensate.
  • Prompting choices are made by folklore. Practitioners pick CoT or few-shot prompts by habit. Whether automated optimization beats a careful manual prompt, and whether code execution beats free-text reasoning for small models, should be measured with proper statistics.
  • Regional conventions are barely tested. The main benchmarks use US filings; recent Indian benchmarks (2026) cover filing look-up [36] and regulatory text [37]. Indian reports use ₹ crore and lakh (1 crore = 100 lakh = 10 million), Indian digit grouping (1,00,000), bracketed negatives and Ind AS statement layouts — multi-step calculation over them is a realistic test of generalization.

4. Objectives and research questions

4.1 Objectives

  1. Benchmark manual prompting strategies (direct, zero/few-shot Chain-of-Thought, Self-Consistency, decomposition, Program-of-Thought) for multi-step financial numerical reasoning.
  2. Evaluate automated prompt optimization (DSPy BootstrapFewShot and MIPROv2, TextGrad): does it beat carefully engineered prompts, and at what one-time cost?
  3. Compare a small and a larger local model (Qwen3-1.7B vs Qwen3-8B; API models supported) on accuracy, tokens, latency and cost per query.
  4. Analyse failure modes and robustness: an error taxonomy, a numerical-perturbation (memorization) test, table-format and retrieval ablations, and generalization to Indian annual reports (₹ crore / lakh).
  5. Propose and test an improvement motivated by the error analysis — Verified Program Self-Consistency (VPSC) — and deliver an interactive web application with evidence and calculation traces.

4.2 Research questions

Research question
RQ1How much do prompting strategies change accuracy on multi-step financial questions, and which is best per token spent?
RQ2Do automatically optimized prompts (DSPy, TextGrad) outperform hand-written prompts, and is the optimization cost justified?
RQ3How do model size (1.7B vs 8B) and strategy interact — can a good strategy close the gap of a smaller model?
RQ4Where do models fail (evidence, units/scale, sign, arithmetic), and are the results robust to changed numbers and to Indian reports?
RQ5Does verifying that a program's numbers are grounded in the report (VPSC) improve over plain program self-consistency?

5. Literature survey

We surveyed work in six themes: (5.1) benchmarks for financial numerical reasoning, (5.2) prompting strategies for multi-step reasoning, (5.3) program-aided reasoning, (5.4) automated prompt optimization, (5.5) tables, retrieval and robustness, and (5.6) domain-specific models. Table 1 at the end of this section summarizes each work and how it is used in this project.

5.1 Financial question-answering benchmarks

FinQA [1] introduced 8,281 question–answer pairs written by financial experts over earnings reports of S&P 500 companies. Each question comes with a gold reasoning program in a small domain-specific language (e.g. subtract(5829, 5735), divide(#0, 5735)) and the supporting facts. The authors' FinQANet (a RoBERTa-large retriever plus a program generator) achieved 61.24% execution accuracy on the test set, compared with 91.16% for human experts, establishing a large gap. FinQA's gold programs let us identify exactly which operands a correct solution needs — we use them for operand-level error analysis and for building numerically perturbed questions.

TAT-QA [2] contains 16,552 questions over 2,757 hybrid contexts (a table plus at least two paragraphs) from real financial reports, with answer types span, multi-span, counting and arithmetic, and a scale label (thousand, million, percent). The proposed TagOp model, which tags evidence cells and applies symbolic aggregation operators, reached 50.1% exact match and 58.0% F1, far below human performance (84.1% EM, 90.8% F1). About 42% of TAT-QA questions are arithmetic; we use this subset. ConvFinQA [3] extends FinQA to conversations in which later questions depend on earlier answers, showing that long chains of numerical reasoning remain difficult. DocFinQA [4] re-poses 7,437 FinQA questions over full filings averaging about 123,000 words, and finds that both retrieval pipelines and long-context LLMs struggle — evidence location is itself a major failure point. FinanceBench [5] evaluates open-book QA over public-company filings and reports that GPT-4-Turbo with retrieval answered 81% of the evaluated questions incorrectly or refused, illustrating that even frontier models are unreliable in this domain. Indian data has only recently been addressed: IndFin-Bench [36] (April 2026, an open dataset released by a company) has 100 questions over Indian listed companies' filings that are mostly fact look-up, and IndiaFinBench [37] (2026) has 406 questions over SEBI and RBI regulatory text, 92 of them numerical. Neither targets multi-step calculation over annual-report statements with ₹ crore/lakh conversions.

Srivastava et al. [8] evaluate LLM mathematical reasoning across TAT-QA, FinQA, ConvFinQA and MultiHiertt, analysing how accuracy changes with table complexity and the number of reasoning steps. They propose EEDP (Elicit → Extract → Decompose → Predict), a prompt that first elicits domain knowledge, then extracts the relevant values, decomposes the computation and predicts the answer; it matches or outperforms CoT and PoT baselines. Our decomposition strategy (Knowledge → Extract → Decompose → Answer) follows this design.

5.2 Prompting strategies for multi-step reasoning

Brown et al. [9] showed that large models can perform tasks from a few in-context examples (few-shot prompting). Wei et al. [10] found that exemplars containing intermediate reasoning steps — Chain-of-Thought (CoT) — strongly improve arithmetic and symbolic reasoning, an ability that emerges with scale. Kojima et al. [11] showed that simply appending “Let's think step by step” elicits reasoning without exemplars (zero-shot CoT), raising InstructGPT's accuracy on MultiArith from 17.7% to 78.7% and on GSM8K from 10.4% to 40.7%. Self-Consistency [12] samples several reasoning paths at non-zero temperature and returns the most frequent answer, improving GSM8K by 17.9 points over greedy CoT. Decomposition methods — least-to-most prompting [13], decomposed prompting [14] and plan-and-solve prompting [35] — first break a problem into sub-problems or an explicit plan, which helps on problems harder than the exemplars. These results come mostly from very large models on math word problems; whether they transfer to small models reading financial tables is an open, empirical question.

5.3 Program-aided reasoning

Free-text reasoning mixes two very different jobs: deciding what to compute and actually computing it. Program-of-Thought (PoT) [15] asks the model to express the reasoning as a Python program and delegates computation to an interpreter. On FinQA with Codex, PoT improved accuracy from 40.4% (CoT) to 64.5%, and from 44.4% to 68.1% with self-consistency; similar gains held on ConvFinQA and TAT-QA. PAL [16] independently showed that program-aided LMs outperform much larger CoT models on math, and Binder [34] binds LMs to SQL/Python for table reasoning. A limitation shared by these methods is that the generated program is trusted completely: if the model copies a wrong number or invents one, the interpreter computes the wrong answer exactly. Two recent works verify numbers: VeriFin [38] grounds the operands of LLM-generated financial claims in filed XBRL facts and checks the calculation with the Z3 solver, reporting zero false accepts; DCRC [39] trains a data-centric agent that audits evidence and compiles executable programs. Both need structured filing data, a solver or task-specific training; plain PoT pipelines over report tables still have no lightweight check that a program's numbers occur in the source.

5.4 Automated prompt optimization

Hand-written prompts are brittle and model-specific, which motivated methods that search for prompts automatically. APE [17] lets an LLM propose candidate instructions and keeps the best-scoring one, matching human-written instructions. OPRO [18] treats the LLM as an optimizer that sees previous prompts with their scores and proposes better ones, outperforming human-designed prompts by up to 8% on GSM8K and up to 50% on Big-Bench Hard. DSPy [19] replaces prompt strings with declarative signatures and modules whose prompts are compiled by optimizers; its BootstrapFewShot optimizer runs the program on training examples and keeps the traces that a metric marks correct as demonstrations. DSPy pipelines outperformed standard few-shot prompting by over 25% (GPT-3.5) and 65% (Llama-2-13B-chat). MIPROv2 [20] jointly optimizes instructions and demonstrations using program- and data-aware instruction proposals, minibatch evaluation and a Bayesian surrogate model, outperforming baselines on five of six programs with Llama-3-8B by up to 13% accuracy. TextGrad [21] generalizes back-propagation to text: an LLM writes natural-language critiques of outputs (“textual gradients”) and an optimizer rewrites the variable (e.g. a system prompt) accordingly; it improved, for example, GPT-4o's zero-shot accuracy on GPQA from 51% to 55%. We found no study that compares these methods with strong hand-written prompts on financial table-text reasoning, or that reports their one-time optimization cost alongside the accuracy gain.

5.5 Tables, retrieval and robustness

How a table is linearized matters: Sui et al. [22] show that the serialization format (e.g. Markdown, HTML, JSON-like formats) and prompt choices significantly change LLM accuracy on structural table tasks, so the format must be treated as an experimental variable. For evidence selection, the classic BM25 ranking function [23] matches keywords, while Sentence-BERT [24] embeddings indexed with FAISS [25] match meaning; Reciprocal Rank Fusion [26] combines rankings simply and robustly. Retrieval-augmented generation [27] grounds outputs in retrieved evidence. On robustness, GSM-Symbolic [28] re-instantiates GSM8K problems with new values and finds that accuracy drops and varies even when only the numbers change — evidence that models partly pattern-match or have memorized public benchmarks. FinQA and TAT-QA are public since 2021, so a similar numerical-perturbation test is needed. Finally, with test sets of a few hundred questions, differences of a few points can be noise; Dror et al. [32] recommend paired significance tests such as McNemar's test [30], with bootstrap confidence intervals [31] and multiple-comparison correction [33].

5.6 Domain-specific models

An alternative to prompting is to build finance-specific models. BloombergGPT [6] is a 50-billion-parameter model trained on a 363-billion-token financial corpus plus 345 billion general tokens. TAT-LLM [7] fine-tunes LLaMA-2-7B on automatically generated extractor–reasoner–executor traces and reports results above GPT-4 on FinQA, TAT-QA and TAT-DQA. These approaches are effective but need curated data, GPU training and retraining for every new base model. General open models such as Qwen3 [29] (0.6B–235B parameters, with switchable thinking and non-thinking modes) improve rapidly; this project studies the cheaper path of prompting and prompt optimization on such models.

Research gaps

  1. No controlled, cost-aware comparison. Prior work reports accuracy for one or two strategies on large proprietary models; token cost, latency and statistical significance across strategies are rarely reported together.
  2. Manual vs automated prompting not compared in finance. DSPy, MIPROv2 and TextGrad were evaluated on general QA, math and code tasks; we found no evaluation on financial table-text reasoning against strong hand-written baselines.
  3. Small local models under-studied. Banks and analysts often cannot send filings to external APIs; how far a 2–8B model on a laptop gets with good prompting is largely unknown.
  4. No Indian calculation benchmark. FinQA, TAT-QA, ConvFinQA and FinanceBench use US/Western reports. The 2026 Indian benchmarks cover filing look-up (IndFin-Bench [36]) and regulatory text (IndiaFinBench [37]), not multi-step calculation with ₹ crore/lakh, 1,00,000 grouping and bracketed negatives.
  5. Errors are counted, not explained. Few studies categorize why answers are wrong or test for memorization of public benchmarks by perturbing numbers (cf. GSM-Symbolic for math).
  6. No lightweight number check. PoT/PAL execute whatever numbers the model writes. Recent verifiers check operands against XBRL facts with a solver (VeriFin [38]) or train an evidence-auditing agent (DCRC [39]); we found no training-free check for plain report tables.

7. Proposed approach

FinReason is built as one controlled pipeline in which only the prompting strategy changes (Fig. 2). Every question flows through the same stages: load into a common format, build the context (full page, or top-k evidence retrieved with a hybrid BM25 + dense retriever), prompt the model, execute any generated code in a sandbox, parse and score the answer, diagnose errors, and record everything for analysis and the web application.

7.1 Strategies compared

  • Manual: direct answer; zero-shot CoT; few-shot CoT (three worked FinQA train examples); decomposition (Knowledge → Extract → Decompose → Answer, after EEDP [8]); Program-of-Thought with sandboxed execution and one repair round; CoT Self-Consistency (5 samples).
  • Automated: DSPy BootstrapFewShot and MIPROv2 compiling a Chain-of-Thought program; TextGrad optimizing the system prompt of zero-shot CoT. All optimization uses FinQA train/dev only, so test sets are never seen.
  • Proposed — Verified Program Self-Consistency (VPSC): sample five programs (one greedy, four at temperature 0.7), execute each in the sandbox, reject programs that use a number not present in the report (allowing harmless constants and unit rescaling), and majority-vote the remaining answers. The same five programs voted without the check form the ablation (PoT Self-Consistency), so the effect of verification is measured exactly at no extra model cost.

7.2 Analyses

  • Accuracy with 95% bootstrap CIs; paired McNemar tests against few-shot CoT with Holm correction.
  • Accuracy stratified by number of reasoning steps and by evidence source (table / text / both).
  • Rule-based error taxonomy: no answer, code failure, sign, scale/unit, near miss, no work shown, evidence error, reasoning error.
  • Numerical perturbation of FinQA questions (operands changed by 10–30%, gold recomputed with the gold program) to test memorization.
  • Ablations: table format (Markdown/JSON/HTML/row text), retrieval vs full page, thinking mode on/off; token cost and latency per strategy.

9. Expected outcomes

  • A reproducible benchmark quantifying accuracy–cost trade-offs of six manual strategies, three optimizers and VPSC across two model sizes.
  • Evidence on whether automated prompt optimization beats careful manual prompting, and at what one-time cost.
  • An error taxonomy and robustness analysis (perturbation, table format, retrieval) showing where LLMs fail in financial calculations.
  • A new curated Indian annual-report test set and a new verification-based strategy (VPSC).
  • An interactive web application with evidence-grounded, auditable answers; a final report in IEEE format.
